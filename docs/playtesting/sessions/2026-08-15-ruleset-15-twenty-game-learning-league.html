LBDesign archive / playtest session

Twenty-game learning league

Four persistent agent identities, twenty complete Ruleset-15 games, eighty postmortems, and cumulative learning between games.

Index/Playtesting/Twenty-game learning league
Completed 15 August 2026

Session and method

Horizon, Vex, Accord, and Cache played twenty complete four-player games through the authenticated game API. Observers were allowed, no timer was used, and every game continued through the third Election. Seat order rotated one position per game, so every identity occupied every seat five times. The server supplied the random agendas and Election draws; completed invitations remain valid for replay observation.

Each persistent player began with a distinct numeric policy. After every game, all four player agents inspected the complete result and wrote a first-person win/loss postmortem. Their numeric adjustments updated their next policy, while every shared lesson entered the common dossier before the subsequent game. The executor refreshed after each Operation card, considered every legal Operation and immediate party bonus, and rejected no commands.

This is an adaptive league, not twenty independent samples. Shared executor improvements were introduced when the dossier exposed systematic blind spots: reference inference and terminal-card valuation after the opening games, and multi-year Organise route readiness after Game 12. Cohort comparisons therefore measure the combined learning system, not a fixed strategy.

Aggregate findings

  • Relative-seat modifiers were the additive component most often aligned with the winner. The winning player had or shared the table-high cumulative modifier in 15 of 20 games. Game 15's +13 Vex modifier was the largest cumulative example; Game 20 was decided entirely by Cache's one-point net modifier advantage over Vex after equal base and Capital totals.
  • Direct agenda scoring remained the most controllable winning foundation. The winner had or shared the highest cumulative base in 13 games. Horizon led the league in both total base and base per ten cards despite finishing behind Vex and Cache on wins, illustrating how the coupled modifier layer can overturn efficient construction.
  • Capital was powerful but unreliable. 39 of 240 player-Election results scored any Capital points, including 7 exact three-point triples. Capital supplied 53 total points. Persistent Centre occupants often became blockers for the next agenda or accidentally completed an opponent's hidden trio.
  • Card ownership enabled action but did not predict victory by itself. Final score correlated 0.01 with cards collected and 0.06 with cards played. Winners led or tied collected cards in only 5 games and cards played in 5. Cache's Game 15 loss—table-high throughput but only four base—is the clearest conversion failure.
  • Whole-pile Collection remained strategically central without reproducing the prior forty-card monopoly. The league recorded 1188 Collections moving 3543 cards. Taking each game's highest player share at any recorded year start, the mean of those game peaks was 52.37%; the worst single snapshot was 60.00%.
  • Dynamic Operate actions remained short. 2510 Operate actions resolved 4420 cards, averaging 1.76. The length distribution was 1206 single-card, 698 two-card, and 606 three-card actions.
  • Route staging and terminal discipline were recurrent execution problems. Early policies passed with large hands when objectives needed intermediate Organise moves, or collected cards in Year 12 when those cards had no future use. Shared learning corrected terminal Collection and added route-readiness value, but wrong-party blockers and incomplete multi-step routes continued to appear in postmortems.
  • Years usually ended by deliberate Closure. 180 of 240 years ended after a majority of parties closed; 60 ended through four consecutive Passes.

HORIZON: won

I won because I survived a poor first Election, kept enough of the closed card economy to remain active despite CACHE's hoarding, and converted a carefully built final agenda into the game's largest single Election swing. My final SC-24 position scored its full six-point district base, the exact three-party Capital for three more, and a favorable three-point relative-seat modifier. ACCORD and CACHE unintentionally completed two of my Capital parties while my Year 7 Many Wings play supplied the third. The victory was decisive, but the relative-seat and opponent-assisted Capital gains mean it was not solely proof that my operation valuation was superior.

Next lessons: Treat the relative-seat term as a first-class scoring objective: my modifiers ranged from -6 to +3, larger than many players' base scores. A future Capital triple is worth seeding early because Centre Support survives every Election, but opponent contributions must be treated as contingent rather than planned certainty.

VEX: Lost, fourth place with 7 points; HORIZON won with 21.

I lost because my activity did not translate into durable agenda scoring. I resolved 33 Operations and collected 23 cards, so this was not simply a lack-of-agency loss, but my three Elections produced only 7 base points, a net -6 relative-seat modifier, and 1 Capital point. Election 1 did the deepest damage: my 2 base was paired with a -6 modifier, dropping me from 5 to 1. I improved my second agenda to 4 base and finally gained 5 points in Election 3, but by then HORIZON converted 6 base, a +3 modifier, and exact three-party Capital presence into a 12-point final Election. My late policy also became too defensive around CACHE's large hand: despite beginning Years 11 and 12 with four cards, I recorded no Operations in either year. Opening Foxglove in both years was aligned with my SC-03 objective, but opening access without using it did not put Foxglove into Ironwood. My Year 10 Riverworks burst targeted Sunmeadow, yet no Riverworks Support survived the final draw there, showing that a thin placement is not enough in a district drawing only two Support. HORIZON beat me by combining better direct base production with a decisive final Capital rather than by monopolizing the card economy.

Next lessons: Track my scoring card's negative seat reference as a first-class threat. A six-point referenced rival base erased three times as much value as my own Election 1 base produced. Direct agenda redundancy matters more than raw Operation count. In a capacity-four district drawing two, one or two desired Support can disappear completely.

ACCORD: lost, third place

I lost because I built the strongest combined district-and-Capital position across the three Elections but failed to convert it through the relative-seat layer. My district bases totaled 15 and my Capital scores totaled 3, compared with HORIZON's 13 and 3, yet my three modifiers were -5, -4, and -3 while HORIZON's summed to zero. HORIZON was the player subtracted from me in every Election and ultimately won, so allowing its base to remain strong was uniquely expensive. I also left the Centre in the Riverworks–Many Wings–Night Parliament configuration that gave me 3 Capital points in Election 2 instead of rebuilding it for my final Foxglove–Night Parliament–Honeycomb set; that stale configuration gave HORIZON its exact final Capital triple. My coalition work preserved a perfect six-point final district base, but it could not compensate for the modifier and Capital swing.

Next lessons: Treat the player named by the negative seat reference as a direct scoring liability, not merely the public score leader; when the same opponent is negative repeatedly, infer its hidden objectives from openings and Operations and disrupt them. After each Election, immediately reevaluate the persistent Centre against the next private Capital set. A previously perfect trio can become three occupied blockers.

CACHE: lost, second place with 14 points; HORIZON won with 21

I lost because I converted card control into too little scoring, especially in the first and final Election cycles. I collected a table-high 38 cards and held 17 cards entering Year 4, 22 entering Year 8, and 18 entering Year 12, but my district bases were only 0, 6, and 3. The opening Election was catastrophic: none of my SC-17 objectives survived the draw and ACCORD's six-point base was my negative reference, so I fell from 5 points to 1. I recovered with the full six-point SC-06 district base and a favorable final relative modifier, but my Centre work yielded only one Capital point across the game. Most damagingly, I passed my only Year 12 turn while still holding 18 cards. HORIZON won by converting its final cycle into six base, exact three-party Capital, and a positive modifier; my resource lead had no terminal value once I failed to make comparable board progress.

Next lessons: Card share is an enabling resource, not a victory condition. Once my hand exceeds what I can plausibly play before the next Election, Collection value must fall sharply and direct agenda conversion must dominate. The final year needs a terminal rule: any legal Operation with positive immediate Election value outranks Collection and Pass because cards left in hand have zero future value.

VEX: Lost, tied for second with CACHE on 16 points; ACCORD won with 27.

I corrected the card-custody failure from Game 1 but still failed to convert actions into my own scoring base. My 17 Collections recovered 40 cards and eight Closures recovered 11 more; I resolved 49 Operations and entered Year 12 with ten cards. That kept me competitive and produced positive relative modifiers in the first two Elections, but my direct bases were only 1, 3, and 1. ACCORD scored bases of 5, 5, and 3 and received +9 modifiers across the game, so it was already seven points ahead after Election 2 and finished eleven ahead. Next lessons: The lower Collection threshold and higher Closure value fixed my agency problem, but card recovery is only useful when followed by agenda-directed play. My current agenda must dominate generic family and bonus value. Operations on an attractive party are wasted if they do not improve one of the three exact district-party objectives or a reference-denial target.

ACCORD: won, first place

I won because I paired strong direct district construction with favorable, deliberately relevant seat relationships and enough Collection pressure to prevent CACHE from monopolizing the card economy. I scored five base points in each of the first two Elections, and the Many Wings–Night Parliament coalition converted two Night draws in Millbank into my Many Wings objective at Election 2. My relative modifiers were +4, +3, and +2 instead of Game 1's three negatives, producing nine of my twenty-two earned points and giving me a lead after every Election. I also collected more cards than CACHE and carried a healthy hand into the final cycle, so the collector never acquired exclusive agency. The eleven-point victory was real, but every player scored zero Capital: I won while leaving a major scoring system unused, and favorable relative-seat alignment contributed heavily.

Next lessons: Relative-seat strategy is conditional, not generic leader denial. Support the positive referenced player's base and suppress the negative referenced player's base for the current card, even when the same opponent changes roles next Election. A reciprocal coalition can be worth several points with very few Court cards: the Night–Many Wings relationship turned two otherwise wrong Millbank draws into SC-14 points.

CACHE: lost, tied for second with 16 points; ACCORD won with 27

I led the table with 55 Operations and collected 43 cards without creating a monopoly, but my three district bases were only 2, 3, and 3. The Game 1 urgency adjustment made me much more active, including five final-year Operations, but I still spent a Year 12 action collecting two cards that could never return to play.

Next lessons: Measure campaign completeness across all three current objectives. Once one objective is near its draw ceiling, its marginal value must fall sharply until the other objectives have credible redundancy. Correct party openings are only access. Before opening, verify that my hand and map topology provide a concrete route to the exact district; after opening, reserve actions to execute that route.

ACCORD: lost, second place

I lost by two points because I converted resources into the strongest direct district total but again allowed relative seats and a badly managed final objective to decide the game. I led CACHE 17–15 before Election 3 and both of us then scored a three-point base with no Capital, but my modifier was -2 while CACHE's was +2. Before committing an election-year objective, identify the opponent's strongest one-action counter. Two matching Support in a district are not redundancy when Stonewall can remove both at once.

CACHE: won with 20 points, ahead of ACCORD on 18, HORIZON on 17, and VEX on 8

Next lessons: Treat this as a seat-reference-assisted win, not proof that five direct base points are sufficient. A repeatable winning policy still needs materially stronger direct scoring. Track whether prepared Support survives until Election timing. Early Foxglove and Many Wings construction was erased before Election 2; late reinforcement, denial of Smear access, or ending the year sooner is required.

Interpretation limits

  • The four identities adapted across games and shared learning, so later games are not independent replications of the early policy.
  • A strategic executor applied numeric policies to exhaustive legal API choices. The subagents shaped those policies and analyzed results, but did not deliberate afresh on every individual command.
  • Private agendas were respected during play. Reference-opponent objectives were inferred from public behavior, then became fully visible only through completed replay.
  • No table talk or negotiated promises occurred. Court coalitions were evaluated from board utility rather than human bargaining.
  • Random deals and Election draws were not seeded by the facilitator. Every completed replay preserves the server's actual setup and draws.
  • Reported correlations are Pearson correlations across the 80 player-game totals. Base, modifier, and Capital are additive terms in final score, so their correlations with score are descriptive decompositions, not independent predictive evidence.
